Notes from the desk.

What we have learned representing creators since 2018. Mostly about money, contracts and the parts of this job nobody puts in a highlight reel.

22 June 2026

Eight years, and the one thing that has not changed

When we started in 2018, a brand deal was a DM and a promise. There was no rate card, no usage window, no clause about whether the video could be cut into an ad two years later. Creators signed things they had not read because reading them was somebody else’s job and there was no somebody else.

Eight years on, almost all of that has changed. Rates are researchable. Contracts are longer and, on the whole, fairer. Platforms have monetisation programmes that pay something close to real money. The industry grew up.

One thing did not change. The person making the work is still the worst-placed person to negotiate for it. Not because they are bad at it, but because they are on the wrong side of the table. You cannot argue your own worth to someone who has to like you, and then go and make a video with them in it. That is the whole reason this company exists, and it is the one part of the job that has never gotten easier.

18 February 2025

Why our roster stays private

Every so often someone asks why we do not list our creators. The short answer is that a roster page is a marketing asset for the agency and a liability for the talent.

Once you are publicly attached to a management company, three things follow. Brands price you by the company’s reputation instead of your own numbers. Other creators on that list become your association, whether you asked for it or not. And the day you leave, the internet notices.

So we do not publish it. Brands get the full list, the real figures and the audience data under NDA, because they need it to make a decision. Everyone else gets a total. Discretion is not a perk we offer, it is the default we work under, and it has never once cost us a deal.

7 August 2023

Five years

LL Media is five years old this month.

In 2018 this was one person, a spreadsheet, and a conviction that creators were being paid roughly a third of what they were worth. The first year was mostly reading other people’s contracts for free to find out how bad they were. They were bad.

Five years later we represent creators across six platforms and negotiate with brands who, in 2018, would not have returned the email. We have argued down exclusivity windows, chased invoices that were ninety days late, killed deals that would have embarrassed the people we represent, and built storefronts for creators who had been told merch was not worth the trouble. It was worth the trouble.

Thank you to every creator who let us handle the ugly half of their business, and to every brand who treated a solo creator like a real media partner. Both of those things were less common in 2018 than they are now, and we are glad to have been slightly wrong about how long it would take.

Here is to the next five.

14 June 2022

Why we started building storefronts

A creator we work with had forty thousand people who would happily have bought a shirt. She did not sell one, because setting up a shop meant choosing a platform, designing the thing, sorting print and shipping, handling returns, and answering emails from people whose order was late. That is a job. She already had one.

So we started doing it. Not as a favour, as a service line. We design the storefront, wire up the checkout, handle fulfilment and restocks, and answer the customer emails. The creator approves the products and sees exactly what each one makes after fees.

It has since grown into its own arm of the company. Sponsorship money is rented; a store you own is yours. Every creator should have both.

4 May 2020

What this year is doing to creator rates

Brand budgets froze in March. Campaigns that were signed got postponed, and campaigns that were verbal disappeared. At the same time, audiences went up across nearly every account we look after, because everyone was at home.

That combination is dangerous. More attention, less money, and a lot of brands quietly offering “exposure” and product instead of a fee, to people whose numbers have never been better.

Our advice to everyone we represent has been the same: do not reset your rate downward in a bad quarter. A rate you drop in a panic takes about two years to climb back. Take fewer deals at your number rather than more deals below it, and spend the spare time on the things you own.

12 November 2018

Why we started LL Media

This company exists because of a contract that arrived on a Friday afternoon.

It offered a creator four figures for a video. It also claimed perpetual worldwide rights to that video for any purpose, blocked her from working with anything in the same category for a year, and had no cap on revisions. She was going to sign it, because it was the most money anyone had offered her and she had nobody to ask.

We read it, sent back six changes, and the brand accepted five of them without argument. The whole thing took an afternoon. The gap between what she was about to accept and what she was actually able to get was not talent, or leverage. It was just having someone whose job it was to read the thing.

That is the entire idea. Creators make the work; somebody else should be handling the paperwork, the pitching and the arguing. We are starting today with a handful of people and a promise that we will never take a deal we would be embarrassed to see posted.
